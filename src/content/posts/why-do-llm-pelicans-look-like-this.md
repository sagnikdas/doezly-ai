Large language models asked to "generate an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle" tend to produce strikingly similar results, and a new post argues this reveals a deeper "diversity collapse" problem in LLM outputs rather than just a quirky benchmark meme. The piece builds on Simon Willison's pelican SVG test, using the original SVGs from his runs at the highest reasoning setting that returned an SVG, and connects the pattern to a new, separately published benchmark on UI design creativity.

![Diagram showing that AI-generated pelican SVGs look nearly identical across models, and a bar chart comparing GPT-6 Astra's Design Creativity Bench score of 65.5 to the human reference score of 92.3.](/assets/posts/why-do-llm-pelicans-look-like-this.svg)

## What is the pelican SVG problem?

The pelican SVG test asks an LLM to generate an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle, and across models the resulting images consistently look alike. The article uses original SVGs from Simon Willison's runs, specifically the highest reasoning setting that returned an SVG, to illustrate this sameness. The author frames this as "the LLM diversity collapse problem no one is talking about," arguing it is not an isolated oddity but a symptom of how LLMs generate creative output in general.

## Why does low output diversity matter for LLM training?

One Hacker News commenter explained that output diversity constraints trace back to a core limitation: LLMs cannot be purely pretrained on other LLMs' outputs because that output lacks diversity. As one commenter on Hacker News put it: "LLM output diversity is low... There is going be no functional difference between hypothetical Qwen 4.5 and Fable 5.5" and "LLMs are not AGI." This suggests that as models increasingly train on data generated by other models, the sameness problem could compound rather than resolve, since all models draw on nearly identical open datasets.

## What did the Design Creativity Bench find about AI design creativity?

Design Creativity Bench, a separate research effort, formalizes this diversity problem by evaluating 16 frontier models across 80 open-ended UI tasks, each adapted to two distinct product domains and compared against human designer references. The headline finding: the best model, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, scores 65.5 against a human reference of 92.3, a substantial creativity gap despite rapid advances in generating functional websites and product UIs. The benchmark measures three dimensions instead of a single output-quality metric:

| Dimension | What it measures |
|---|---|
| Originality | How distinct a model's designs are from other models' outputs for the same brief |
| Creative Range | How much a model adapts its design decisions when the product domain changes |
| Appropriateness | How well the generated UI satisfies the functional requirements of the brief |

The full leaderboard, methodology, and reproducible evaluation framework are published on Hugging Face Datasets and GitHub, intended to make design creativity a measurable dimension of frontier model capability.

## Is this just a prompting problem?

Several Hacker News commenters pushed back, arguing that diversity is achievable with better prompts rather than being a fundamental model flaw. One commenter noted, "It is fairly easy to go ask for a differently styled pelican, environment, background," while another asked, "If the design looks too generic, you can just ask for a change, no?" Another commenter was more skeptical of LLM creativity itself, describing the model as "a predictive text model with a fixed set of data... It's a spell checker, not an art student," pointing to the tension between randomness (which caused hallucination) and consistency (which caused sameness) as a core tradeoff labs have already tuned against.

## What are the practical implications for developers?

For developers building on LLMs, the sameness problem has a concrete business angle: one commenter pointed out that because "make me a thing that does X" tends to produce near-identical implementations across users, it becomes "trivial" to reverse-engineer a competitor's design limitations just by prompting an LLM the same way. This reinforces the Design Creativity Bench's core finding that current frontier models still fall well short of human designers, at least by the numbers (65.5 vs. 92.3), when it comes to generating genuinely original, context-adapted design decisions rather than converging on the statistically likely answer.
