Some of the same banks that raced to underwrite AI data center loans six months ago are now trying to get that debt off their books. JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, SMBC and MUFG have been exploring significant risk transfers — deals that let a bank keep a loan on paper while shifting its riskiest slice to outside investors — because they're bumping up against internal limits on how much AI-linked exposure any one lender can hold, according to a Financial Times report.

## What exactly are the banks trying to do?

Banks including JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, SMBC and MUFG are exploring significant risk transfers to shift the riskiest slices of AI data center loans to outside investors while keeping the loans nominally on their books. The clearest example: a group of banks including JPMorgan and MUFG has spent more than six months trying to distribute $38 billion in construction debt tied to a Texas and Wisconsin data center project leased to Oracle, per Bloomberg. Some lenders are now willing to sell pieces of it to non-bank buyers at a discount, just to move it — a sign the trade has gotten stuck.

## Why is Oracle at the center of this?

Oracle is the clearest tell of stress in this market: S&P cut its credit rating to BBB-, one notch above junk, in July, and its five-year credit default swaps climbed to around 151 basis points in early October — levels last seen in 2009 — before later hitting 203 basis points, an 18-year high. Oracle is reportedly targeting more than $40 billion in additional debt and equity this fiscal year, on top of the $18 billion in bonds it sold in a single day last September, all to keep funding its Texas, Wisconsin and New Mexico sites.

Oracle also sued Wisconsin's Public Service Commission over a rule that could force it to post more than $7 billion in collateral for its Port Washington data center campus — a dispute that helped push the S&P downgrade. Meta's latest bond, meanwhile, drew a third less investor demand than its sale a year earlier.

## How much has demand for this debt actually dropped?

Order books for AI-related bonds from Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle have shrunk from roughly five times the amount on sale back in February to under two times by July, according to Bloomberg. Bonds from these companies now trade at a premium to similarly rated non-AI corporate debt — the difference, as the source material puts it, "between a bond that sells itself and one a bank has to work to place."

The pricing gap is sharper for weaker credits. Capital is rotating toward data centers leased to investment-grade hyperscalers like Microsoft and Google, which is why CoreWeave-leased paper prices at 7.00% to 9.875%, against the 5.70% to 7.875% that hyperscaler-leased bonds command, per bond market data compiled by CCIR.

## Is this connected to the broader bond market, or just AI-specific?

Yes, both: the 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.304% this month, its highest since 2002, after a $39 billion auction cleared at 5.30%, the highest auction yield since 2000, and the Federal Reserve's September meeting minutes named AI-related corporate borrowing as a contributor to that move. Capital Economics disputes that framing, arguing inflation and government deficits are the bigger driver — but either way, the risk-free rate that data center financing gets measured against just got a lot more expensive.

Matthew Moniot, co-head of credit risk sharing at Man Group, told the Financial Times the deal sizes involved are "out of scale to anything we've thought about, ever" — notable because it's someone whose job is pricing exactly this kind of risk saying the numbers don't fit any prior model.

## Why does this matter beyond one bank's balance sheet?

Banks sell down debt when they're worried about concentration, and "approaching internal limits" means too much of a bank's balance sheet is now one bet on one sector holding up. Spreading roughly $120 billion of AI infrastructure spending across special purpose vehicles — the structure Oracle, Meta, xAI and CoreWeave have all used to keep debt off their own balance sheets — was supposed to make that risk easier to absorb. Instead, the risk is showing up at the banks that financed those SPVs in the first place, and a smaller, less capitalized AI infrastructure company raising debt next quarter is now competing for the same nervous pool of lenders as Oracle, without Oracle's balance sheet or customer base behind it.

One commenter on Hacker News noted the broader pattern: "AI debt issuance fell 50% month over month in September," pointing to a drop from a record $113 billion in June to $23 billion in September. Another was blunter about what's at stake: "Who is building the infrastructure to resell the gpus from failed data centers if / when this collapses."
