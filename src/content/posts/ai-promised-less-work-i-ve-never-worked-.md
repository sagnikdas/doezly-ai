An engineer who now runs multiple AI agents in parallel is logging more GitHub activity than at any point in his career, despite holding a senior leadership role that should mean less hands-on coding, not more.

![Bar chart comparing yearly GitHub contribution counts from 2020-2023 and partial-year 2026, highlighting August 2026 as the busiest month ever recorded.](/assets/posts/ai-promised-less-work-i-ve-never-worked-.svg)

## What is the article actually about?

The post is a first-person analysis by a full-stack engineer turned VP of Technology at Bold.org, who pulled his GitHub contribution history from January 2019 to present to understand why AI tools made him busier instead of freeing up time. He isn't complaining about the tools themselves — he uses agents for all his engineering work now — but found that running several agents in parallel, each occasionally needing input, creates a new, more mentally draining kind of work than the two or three serious tasks he used to juggle per week.

## What do the GitHub numbers actually show?

The data traces a clear arc: high activity during his early Full Stack Engineer years, a steep drop during his Principal Engineer and early VP years, then an unexplained surge back to peak levels in 2026 after adopting AI agents. Here's the year-by-year contribution count:

| Period | Role | Contributions |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | Full Stack Engineer | 2,711 (highest year) |
| 2021 | Full Stack Engineer | 1,839 |
| 2022 | Full Stack Engineer → Principal | 1,498 |
| 2023 | Principal Engineer | 1,189 (lowest year) |
| Aug 2022–Jul 2025 avg/month | Principal Engineer | 114 |
| Aug–Dec 2025 avg/month | VP of Technology | 111 |
| Dec 2025 | VP of Technology | 50 (quietest month) |
| Jan 1–Oct 27, 2026 | VP of Technology | 2,102 |

By comparison, Full Stack Engineer years averaged 173 contributions a month versus 114 as Principal Engineer. In 2026, the median weekday jumped to 7 contributions, close to the 8 he logged in 2020, up from 3–4 every year from 2022–2025. Weekdays with zero GitHub activity fell to 18 (through October 27, 2026) versus 40–46 in each full year from 2022–2025. Days with 24+ contributions went from exactly one per year (2022–2025) to 16 so far in 2026 — nearly matching the 17 he had in all of 2020.

## Why is August 2026 the standout month?

August 2026 was the single busiest month in his entire six-plus-year dataset, with 427 contributions — well above the previous high of 319 set in April 2020. The spike came right after a full week of zero recorded contributions in early August (his summer break), followed by elevated activity every weekday from August 10 to August 27. Earlier in the year, January (277) and February (233) formed the first major burst, coinciding with when he started working seriously with agents and building an internal company agent from an observability experiment. April (187) was a smaller secondary burst, which he attributes partly to model improvements, and included a single day with 29 contributions plus his year's highest public-contribution total of 68.

## Why is his role a twist in this story?

The irony is that his current job as VP of Technology should mean doing far less hands-on feature work than he did in 2020 as a Full Stack Engineer, yet his GitHub graph for 2026 looks like his busiest coding year ever. He explains this by noting that cheap, AI-assisted coding changed what counts as "worth doing" — bugs he'd have ignored, codebase-wide pattern improvements, and new projects all became feasible, expanding his workload rather than shrinking it. Almost all of the 2026 increase is private work: public contributions were near zero from 2021–2025 and reached only 191 so far in 2026, still less than a tenth of his total output for the year.

## What does the Hacker News discussion add?

The top comment on Hacker News frames it as an inevitable efficiency trap: "It's like an unevitable escalation. Such putting your pedal on full gas without knowing how to leave it from there." Another commenter pushed back on the article's premise entirely, arguing: "Realistically there were only ever two possible outcomes: you do more work for the same amount of money, or you're one of the ones who were laid off."
