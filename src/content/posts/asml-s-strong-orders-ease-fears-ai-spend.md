ASML's forecast of Q4 sales above expectations, tied to AI chip demand, is easing worries that AI infrastructure spending is slowing down. The original Reuters article text was not available for this post, so the following is based on the headline, URL, and the Hacker News discussion it sparked (116 points, 100 comments).

![A side-by-side timeline comparing chip-equipment order booms in 2000 and 2026, questioning whether ASML's strong Q4 forecast is a genuine new demand signal or déjà vu of a past cycle that preceded a bust.](/assets/posts/asml-s-strong-orders-ease-fears-ai-spend.svg)

## What did ASML report?

Based on the Reuters headline and URL, ASML forecast Q4 2026 sales above expectations, citing AI chip demand as a driver. No further figures (exact sales numbers, order backlog size, or customer names) were available in the source material provided.

## Why does ASML's order book matter for the AI spending debate?

ASML makes the lithography machines used to manufacture advanced chips, so strong orders are often read as a signal that chipmakers expect sustained AI demand. But commenters on Hacker News pushed back on treating ASML orders as a real-time demand gauge, since the machines ordered today produce chips that won't reach the market for years.

One commenter on Hacker News put it this way: "That's like adding power plants to the grid that runs the ball pump inflating the ballon." Another argued the asymmetry of risk favors over-ordering: "if you don't buy the machines in time, mispredict the demand and can't produce, you lose tens of billions."

## Is a long order backlog unusual for ASML?

No — several commenters pointed out that ASML has historically had a years-long backlog regardless of the broader semiconductor cycle. One commenter asked directly: "Aren't they always selling everything they manufacture? No matter if you are in a boom or a bust, ASML will always have a years long backlog." This raises the question of whether a strong Q4 forecast is genuinely new information or simply business as usual for the company.

## Does this echo past capex cycles?

Commenters drew a direct parallel to the dot-com era. One linked a February 2000 EE Times piece headlined "Record orders for chip gear push SEMI index to four-year high," which reported that chip equipment suppliers like Applied Materials were raising their 2000 revenue growth forecasts as manufacturers added new wafer fabs — a boom that preceded the dot-com bust. The comparison suggests that record capital equipment orders have historically not been a reliable leading indicator of sustained end-demand.
